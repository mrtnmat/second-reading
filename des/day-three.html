Day three: bit for bit, and back to the boss

Demon's Souls, the journal, part 4 · revised 2026-11-04 · draft

In short

At 01:10 on 4 November, a full movement step of the player was computed entirely by our own code, with all collision included. On every recording we had, it matched the game bit for bit measured.

By the next afternoon the work had returned to the boss that was dropped at five in the morning the day before. This time it was built from parts we can measure.

Asked how healthy the project was, the session working on the attacks gave an honest answer: strong evidence, narrow coverage.

How it went

One routine at a time

Through the night, one Havok routine after another was written out by hand and checked against the game, each with an exact score:

  • the ground check, which finds the floor under the character;
  • the walk through Havok's compressed collision tree: 22,500 of 22,500 steps identical;
  • the constraint solver, which settles all of a frame's contacts at once;
  • the closest point between the character's capsule and a triangle of the level.

Collision trees and solvers

A game level is made of many thousands of small triangles. Testing the character against every one of them each frame would be far too slow.

So the triangles are sorted in advance into a tree: the whole level is cut into smaller regions, each region into smaller ones again, and so on, down to single triangles. To find what the character might touch, the engine starts at the top and only follows the regions the character overlaps. A few dozen steps instead of thousands of tests. Havok also packs this tree tightly to save memory, which makes it harder to read.

A character often touches several things at once: the floor, a wall, the corner of a step. Pushing it out of each one separately can push it into another. A constraint solver looks at all the contacts together and finds one movement that satisfies all of them as well as it can.

01:10

Then came the milestone. On the recorded walks, a full movement step, with all collision computed by our own code, came out bit for bit equal to the game measured.

What "bit for bit" means

Computers store numbers like positions in a fixed number of binary digits, bits. Most fractions can't be stored exactly, so every calculation rounds a little.

That rounding depends on the exact order of the steps. Work out a + b + c as (a + b) + c or as a + (b + c), and the last bit of the answer can differ. A tiny difference, but over hundreds of frames tiny differences grow.

So "close" is not enough proof that we understand the code. "Bit for bit" means every bit of every number equals the game's: the same steps, in the same order, with the same rounding. Getting there is strong evidence that we rebuilt the routine the way it really is.

Walking, turning, and the loop

The code that chooses the walking animation (walk, run, dash, and when to switch) was rebuilt next.

By morning it ran in a closed loop with the physics step. The animation drives the movement, and the movement feeds the next choice of animation.

The 180-degree turn was reproduced too.

Open loop and closed loop

There are two ways to test a piece of a simulation.

In an open loop, each frame starts from the real game's numbers. We feed our code the game's state, compare its answer for the next frame, then throw that answer away and start the next frame from the game's numbers again. A mistake can't grow, so it is easy to find.

In a closed loop, our code runs on its own answers. Each frame starts where our previous frame ended. That is the real test: if anything is slightly wrong, the error carries forward and grows. A closed loop that stays on track for a whole recording means the parts really fit together.

No peeking ahead

The owner set one more rule that night.

A simulation may take events from a recording, but only on the frame they really happened, and labelled as such:

That makes the difference between a causal simulation with known external events and a replay that has advance knowledge.

Simulation versus replay

Imagine a weather model that is allowed to read tomorrow's newspaper. Its forecasts would be perfect, and worthless.

A simulation can cheat the same way. If our code can look at what the recording says happens next, it can match the game without understanding anything. The rule keeps it honest: an outside event, such as the player pressing a button, may enter the simulation only on the frame it happened, never earlier.

Back to the Vanguard, from below

By the morning of the third day, the work had returned to the boss, this time from the bottom up:

  • The player's hit reactions to the Vanguard's blows went through our walking and physics model, and stayed within a few millionths of a metre of the game.
  • The Vanguard's attack shapes were placed through its skeleton, the way the game does it.
  • Its body was the first character to collide with the player in our model.
  • The checker for which attacks may hit matched all 2,301 recorded decisions.

Skeletons and hit shapes

Characters in 3D games are animated with a skeleton: an invisible set of bones, each attached to the one before it, like shoulder, elbow, wrist. The animation moves the bones, and the visible body follows them.

A blow doesn't hit because the picture of the club touches the player. Each attack carries invisible hit shapes, usually spheres or capsules, fixed to bones. On each frame of the swing, the game moves them with the bone and tests them against the player's body. To place them right, we have to move the skeleton the way the game does.

Footsteps

A sound session joined in. It decoded all 139 sound banks (3,295 clips) and predicted which footstep sound plays from the floor material in our collision map.

Footsteps driven by our own simulated movement matched all 31 in the recording measured.

How healthy is it?

At the end of the window, the owner asked the session working on the attack logic how healthy the project was. Its answer was candid:

  • The evidence is good. Every claim carries an address and a status.
  • The coverage is narrow. The 2,301 matches come from one recording, one enemy and one map. That is strong evidence for that recording, and weak evidence for the system.
  • Waiting costs time. Only one copy of the game can run at a time, and three sessions take turns.

The suggested next step was a different recording, not more checks on the same one. The owner agreed, and restated the goal:

We are optimizing for a trustworthy independent Vanguard simulation, not generalizing every game subsystem.

The boss simulation that was dropped at 05:16 on day two is the goal again.

Evidence and coverage

A check can be strong and still narrow.

Matching 2,301 of 2,301 decisions is strong: not one disagreement. But all of them come from one walk through one fight. They show that our code agrees with the game in the situations that recording happened to contain. A situation the recording never met, such as a different weapon or a different floor, hasn't been tested at all.

That is why the next step is a new recording rather than more checks on the old one. More checks on the same data add little. New situations show where the model breaks.
