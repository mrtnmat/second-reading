Walls you can walk through, and fences you can't see

King's Field, read from the code, part 6 · revised 2026-10-23 · draft

Spoilers: this article names a secret room on the first floor and what is inside it.

In short

King's Field hides rooms behind walls that aren't really there. The map underneath marks them as ordinary floor; only the picture says "wall". Walk straight at one and you pass through, with no button to press.

There is a magic item, the Phantom Rod, that makes these walls flicker for a while and show what they really are.

While investigating one of these secret rooms we found a second, separate trick: invisible fences. Hundreds of floor squares, at room entrances and corridor junctions, are marked "no monsters". You walk across them without noticing; monsters can't. That is why creatures stay in their own areas instead of following you through the dungeon.

How it works

A wall that is only a picture

Every square of a floor stores separately what it looks like and how you collide with it (see A 3D world on a 2D grid). Usually the two agree. A hidden wall is a square whose looks say "wall" and whose collision says "open floor" in the code.

The first one we checked is on floor 1, west of where you start: a passage that looks like the corridor's wall, flanked by two real wall pieces. A script that only pushed forward on the controller walked the player straight through it into a small room measured. The room holds a chest with a Small Shield in the code, and a skeleton.

Other squares on other floors use the same "looks like wall, is floor" piece. We can find them all in the map data, but not all of them have been walked through yet in the code. Not every secret in the game works this way, either: doors, moving walls and passages that open on conditions are separate mechanisms.

The Phantom Rod

Using the Phantom Rod starts a timer of 1,000 updates, 50 seconds in the code. While it runs, the game swaps the three hidden-wall pieces for different-looking ones when it draws the floor, but only on half of the updates, so the walls flicker between their disguise and their true look in the code. The rod also tints the whole view while it lasts in the code. Collision is never touched: the rod only changes what you see.

The skeleton that wouldn't follow

A player on our team noticed that the skeleton in that secret room never comes out after you, even though you can walk in and out freely. Several explanations were possible: maybe monsters can't cross hidden walls, maybe it was the skeleton's size, or its way of finding a path. We wrote them all down and tested before picking one.

The answer was none of those. The square just inside the hidden wall carries a flag that monsters can't cross in the code. With the player standing outside, the skeleton chased and swung at them, and stopped dead at that line every time measured. The hidden wall itself had nothing to do with it.

Invisible fences everywhere

Once we knew what to look for, the fences turned out to be everywhere: between 139 and 320 fenced squares on each floor, at corridor junctions and room entrances in the code. The game's collision check compares a square's flags with the flags of whatever is moving. Monsters and other characters carry the "fence" flag, so they are stopped; the player doesn't, so they pass in the code.

It is a cheap and invisible way to keep each creature in its area, the level designer's equivalent of a sheepdog our reading. It probably also explains why stepping back through a doorway so often ends a chase our reading.

From here on it gets technical: formulas, memory addresses and how each claim was checked.

The details

The hidden wall on floor 1

Floor 1, tile (4,16): tile type 0x45, collision class 1 (open floor), direction 2, between solid tile types 0x44 and 0x46 in the code. Container 13 at (4,20) holds the Small Shield in the item data in the code.

Crossing check (2026-10-11, DuckStation): after a warp to the approach, forward input only; x stays 9,000 while z goes from 30,500 to 35,660, across the whole entrance tile (z 32,000–33,999), with no interaction button measured. That the passage looks like a wall is the human player's observation, recorded with screenshots from the approach and from inside.

Phantom Rod

kf_UseItem (80018054), item 0x39: timer 800a07d2 = 1,000, counted down once per update in kf_PlayerUpdate. The tile renderer (8001e5ec) substitutes types 44 → 17, 45 → 18 and 46 → 19 while the timer is active and its low two bits are 0 or 1: two updates on, two off in the code. The view tint is drawn through FUN_800187f0. The darkness status is a different effect: a real hit from a floor-2 Phantom never reached the tint call over 544 frames measured.

Every passable type-0x45 square can be listed from the map data with its reveal replacement; only the floor-1 one is movement-verified.

Creature fences

Map layer 3, bit 0x80, the only zone bit used on the disc: 145 / 209 / 139 / 231 / 320 tiles on floors 1–5. kf_CheckCollision (8001a5ac) step 5 returns a block when the tile's high flag bits match the mover's collision flags (flags >> 8). Monsters and other characters move with 0x8040 or 0x8060; the player with 0x881 in the code.

Check (DuckStation): the secret-room skeleton at (4,18), whose entrance tile (4,17) is fenced, chased and swung with the player outside but never crossed z 36,000 measured. The question was first recorded on 2026-10-11 with four candidate causes and resolved on 2026-10-12.

kf_CheckCollision returns a block for any matching zone bit, so a fenced square stops a monster wherever it is placed. A handful of monsters are placed on squares that refuse every step of their own (diagonal half-tiles or fenced tiles), so they would turn on the spot in the code. That has not yet been watched in the game.
