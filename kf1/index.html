King's Field, read from the code

draft

King's Field was one of the very first PlayStation games, released by FromSoftware in Japan in December 1994, a few weeks after the console itself. (The game sold in the West as King's Field is the second one in the series.) It is a slow first-person dungeon crawler, and it is famously unforgiving about explaining itself.

We have been taking its program apart. This series explains what we found: how the game really works underneath, especially the parts nobody could be sure about from playing alone. Each article starts in plain language and gets more technical as it goes; stop wherever you like.

  1. 1A 3D world on a 2D grid · draft

    Every floor is a 100 × 100 grid of squares, like a board game seen from the inside. Walls, caves, stairs, and even how far you can see all follow from that grid.

  2. 2Why it feels heavy · draft

    The famous slow, deliberate feel of King's Field is a handful of numbers: 20 updates a second, momentum on every move, and a turn that takes over seven seconds.

  3. 3Why the text is pictures · draft

    Almost every line of text in King's Field is a picture loaded from the disc, not letters drawn by the program. A tiny font of about 115 kanji covers the rest, and it was built from the game's own word list.

  4. 4How combat really works · draft

    One small formula decides every hit, yours and the monsters'. Strength works on both sides of it, armour is counted oddly, and poison resistance is a plain percentage.

  5. 5Levels, training and the level-48 ceiling · draft

    The level table stops at 40, but the game keeps going by repeating its last step, until the experience counter fills up at level 48. Strength and magic also grow by practice, one point per hundred real hits.

  6. 6Walls you can walk through, and fences you can't see · draft

    Some walls are only pictures: the map marks them as open floor. And monsters stay in their rooms because of invisible fences that stop them but not you.
